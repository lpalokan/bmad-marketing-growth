# House style

The dossier is read by an Account Executive, often quickly, often just before a
call. Write for a capable colleague who does not know this account.

## The register

**Professional and simple.** Simple means short sentences, plain words and one
idea at a time. It does not mean talking down. An AE reading this is a
professional, and copy that sounds like a children's book fails as badly as copy
nobody can parse.

Test: could you say this sentence out loud to the buyer without embarrassment.

## Sentence rules

- Short sentences by default. Vary the length so the prose keeps moving.
- One idea per paragraph. Three to five sentences.
- Expand jargon the first time it appears. Second line, control testing, straight
  through processing, delegated authority. Assume the reader knows selling and
  not the account's industry.
- Prefer the concrete noun to the abstraction. "Evidence a control was operating"
  beats "audit-defensible traceability by construction".
- Put the subject early and the verb next.

## Prohibited constructions

- **No em dashes and no en dashes.** Use a period, a comma, a colon or brackets.
- **No "X, not Y".** Split it into two sentences or rewrite it positively.
  Weak: "The finding was about risk management, not the code."
  Better: "The finding was about risk management. The code was almost incidental."
- **No mid-sentence interrupters** splitting a main clause, above all between
  subject and verb. Trailing clauses and ordinary lists are fine.
- **No headline that labels a topic.** Headlines are complete sentences in active
  voice, with the audience as the subject where possible.
- **No leading questions.** A question carrying its own conclusion buys a
  confirmation, and a confirmation is worth nothing. Rewrite it open and neutral
  so the reader reaches the conclusion themselves. This governs the discovery
  questions in section 6 and any question inside a message.
  Leading: "Control testing is your big priority this year, right?"
  Neutral: "Where does control testing sit against your other priorities?"

## Emphasis

**Bold does not appear in body text.**

The dossier already carries a hierarchy: headings, tables, block quotes and
lists. Bold competes with all four. A bolded sentence opening a paragraph reads
as a heading, so the reader loses track of where sections begin. The page stops
being scannable at exactly the moment it looks most scannable.

Bold is allowed in three places and nowhere else.

- The label column of a key and value table, where it is the table's structure
  rather than prose.
- The `KNOW`, `ASSUME` and `GAP` labels, which are a controlled vocabulary the
  reader scans for.
- The `[PROOF NEEDED]` flag, for the same reason.

Everything else stays unbolded. That includes company names, figures, dates, the
recommendation, the person to approach, and the one sentence you most want read.

### What to reach for instead

A sentence that needs to stand out has a structural home. Find it.

| You want to | Use |
|---|---|
| Announce what a block of text is about | A heading, written as a complete sentence |
| Make one claim impossible to miss | A block quote, at most once per section |
| Set several things against each other | A table |
| Put a fact first | The first sentence of the paragraph |

If none of those fit, the sentence is ordinary prose and it will be read.

### Italics

Reserved for the standing instruction blocks the template carries, such as the
"examples, illustrative" note and the "why we ask" line under each discovery
question. Never used for emphasis.

### Headings carry the structure

Headings are the only device that tells a reader where they are. Give every
heading a level that matches its depth, and never skip a level to reach a size
you like. A bolded lead-in doing a heading's job is a defect. Promote it to a
heading or demote it to a sentence.

## Lists

Bullet lists take a period on the last item only. Keep items parallel in shape.

## Analogies

Welcome, and they work in sales. Rules:

- At most one analogy carries the insight. More than one competes with itself.
- It must be businesslike. Reach for due diligence, an inspection regime, an
  insurance policy, a supply chain. Avoid whimsy and avoid domestic scenes unless
  the account's own structure makes one exact.
- The analogy has to survive contact with the buyer's world. If a CRO would find
  it patronising, cut it.

## Numbers

- Write figures the way the source writes them, with the unit. EUR 110.8 million,
  not 110.8m, unless a table forces brevity.
- Ratios keep the source's form. If they report 0.71, do not convert to 71%.
- Always give the comparative. A number without a prior period is not an argument.
- Never round in a way that flatters our case.

## The anti-metric rule

**A style rule is never a metric.**

Do not measure compliance with this file by counting sentence lengths, running a
readability score, or grepping for banned strings and declaring success. Those
counts are symptoms. Optimising them produces choppy, primer-like prose that
passes the check and reads worse.

Short sentences are what happens when you start inside the subject and know what
you are saying. They are not a target to hit by chopping.

The grep is a backstop for the mechanical rules only: dashes, "X, not Y",
terminal periods in headlines. Everything else is judged by reading it.

## Naming and tone toward the account

- Never imply the account failed, even when the public record says it did. Frame
  as extending coverage rather than as fixing blame.
- Never use headcount reduction language. The line is freeing skilled people for
  judgement work.
- Do not pitch a scale the account does not have. A mid-sized bank knows it is not
  a global bank and will hear the mismatch.
- Where an account's stated ambition conflicts with an offering, say so in the
  dossier and route around it.
